\section{Experiments}
\subsection{Sverdrup circulation}
Neglecting the Munk theory and only focusing on the Sverdrup solution gives \autoref{fig:f_plane_streamfunction}--\ref{fig:streamfunction_domain_compare}. One can in \autoref{fig:f_plane_streamfunction} see the result if only the $f$-plane is assumed. There one can see that the solution does not resemble the theory presented by Sverdrup. Here the solution is symmetric, which makes sense since there is no latitudinal asymmetry caused by the $\beta$-term. The observed solution in \autoref{fig:f_plane_streamfunction} is thus simply a geostrophic solution to the wind stress, and thus symmetric in the domain. 

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[height=0.3\textwidth]{Figures/no_drag_f_plane_streamfunction.png}
    \caption{Streamfunction for the no-drag $f$-plane configuration. The circulation remains symmetric with no $\beta$-effect and without western intensification.}
    \label{fig:f_plane_streamfunction}
\end{figure}

If one introduces the $\beta$-term one obtains results in \autoref{fig:u10_comparison}. \autoref{fig:u10_comparison}--\ref{fig:domain_compare_cross} uses a time averred solution, since a steady state was never observed fro the Sverdrup circulation. Thus without the time averaging (which can be seen in \autoref{fig:munk_comparison}) one can see a a large discrepancy. However, when taking the time average, a more Sverdrup-like solution can be observed. 

In \autoref{fig:u10_comparison} one can observe more Sverdrup like solution compared to \autoref{fig:f_plane_streamfunction}, due to the asymmetric beta effect (stronger $f$ northward and weaker southward) pushing of the solution towards the western boundary. The symmetric solution is thus broken and a better Sverdrup solution is obtained. 

Varying the magnitude of the wind stress can be done by varying the ten meter wind as seen in \autoref{fig:u10_comparison}. There one can see the effect of a stronger wind stress on the Sverdrup circulation. It is clear that increasing the wind also increases the flux of the domain. This makes sense since the wind stress is the forcing that drives this circulation. Thus having more forcing should yield larger values of the streamfunction. 

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[height=0.3\textwidth]{Figures/u10_comparison.png}
    \caption{Comparison of streamfunction fields for different ten meter wind speeds. Increasing wind speeds shows that it increases the magnitude of the wind-driven ocean gyre. The contours show the model result, while the solid red lines showcase the Sverdrup theory. The time averaging was done between two days and two weeks for the model.}
    \label{fig:u10_comparison}
\end{figure}

The cross sectional streamfunction for varying ten meter wind can be seen in \autoref{fig:u10_cross_section}. There one can see that an increased wind shear (ten meter wind) yields a stronger meridional velocity. However, \autoref{fig:u10_cross_section} also shows the agreement to the theoretical result, where one can see good agreement for the time-averaging. Away from the western boundary the model and the theory align very good, but close to the western boundary they are very different. This discrepancy arises from the mass conservation of the numerical model. Since the theoretical Sverdrup is not in closed form, but goes from the top down to the bottom, there will be mass build up at this lower point. This was not a problem in the theoretical model since a steady state solution was assumed, but here we have time steps which highlight this difference. Thus due to mass conservation, the theoretical result will be impossible for the numerical model to mimic. However, far away from the boundary, the theoretical and numerical models still align very good. 

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[height=0.5\textwidth]{Figures/u10_cross_section.png}
    \caption{Zonal cross-sections of the streamfunction at the center of the domain for different ten meter winds. Solid lines show the numerical model results, while dashed lines show Sverdrup theory. A large difference between the model and the theory can be observed. The meridional wind velocity has been transformed into an Ekman-layer velocity, by assuming an Ekman depth of $\delta_\text{EK}=\SI{50}{\meter}$. The meridional velocity is thus the corresponding mass flux as if the domain is at rest and only the Ekman layer is moving. The time averaging was done between two days and two weeks for the model.}
    \label{fig:u10_cross_section}
\end{figure}

The model ran on parameters resembling the North Atlantic, but changing this to a North Pacific scenario yielded \autoref{fig:streamfunction_domain_compare}--\ref{fig:domain_compare_cross}. There one can observe the effect if one increases the domain width, while keeping $\delta x$ constant. Doing this shows the effect the domain width has on the solution. Observing \autoref{fig:streamfunction_domain_compare} one can see that we have a larger flux for the North Pacific compared to the North Atlantic. Observing \autoref{fig:domain_compare_cross} one can see the effect of the domain increase on the accuracy. On the larger Pacific one can see that the solution is still bad in the western boundary, but away from the western boundary the solution is just as good as for the North Atlantic. One can also note that the increased domain pushed the peak and western boundary current closer to the western boundary.

Overall, clear limitations of the Sverdrup model can be observed due to the accumulation of mass along the western boundary. This leads to the formation of a strong western boundary current, which is not represented in the original Sverdrup theory. Therefore, some form of viscosity or friction must be included in the system to properly balance the boundary transport. Another clear limitation is the fact that the solution does not reach stead state (as seen in \autoref{fig:munk_comparison}), which forces one to do a time-averaging to get realistic result.
